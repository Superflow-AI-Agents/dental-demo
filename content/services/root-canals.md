## What a root canal is

Inside every tooth is a soft core called the pulp, made of nerves and blood vessels. It runs down through thin channels in the roots. If deep decay, a crack, or an injury lets bacteria reach the pulp, it becomes inflamed or infected. It cannot heal on its own, and the infection can spread into the bone at the tip of the root.

Root canal treatment removes the damaged pulp, cleans and disinfects the channels, and seals them. The tooth stays in place and keeps working. An adult tooth does not need its pulp to survive once it is fully grown, because the gum and bone around it keep it alive.

## Signs you might need one

<ul class="check-list">
<li>A toothache that throbs or keeps you awake</li>
<li>Sensitivity to hot or cold that lingers after the drink is gone</li>
<li>Pain when you bite or press on a tooth</li>
<li>A tooth that has turned gray or darker</li>
<li>Swollen, tender gum near a tooth, or a small pimple on the gum</li>
</ul>

Sometimes there are no symptoms at all, and we find the problem on an x-ray. If you have swelling in your face, call us the same day. See [emergency dentistry](/services/emergency-dentistry).

## What to expect, step by step

Most root canals take one visit of 60 to 90 minutes. Molars, which have more roots, can take longer or need a second visit.

1. **X-rays and numbing.** We confirm which tooth is the cause, then numb it fully. If the tooth is badly inflamed we may give extra anesthetic and wait until it takes.
2. **Isolation.** We place a thin rubber sheet around the tooth. It keeps the area clean and dry and stops anything going down your throat.
3. **Cleaning the canals.** Through a small opening in the top of the tooth, we remove the pulp and shape each canal with fine instruments, rinsing with disinfecting solution as we go.
4. **Sealing.** We fill the canals with a rubber-like material and seal the opening with a filling.

If a case is unusually complex, such as curved or blocked canals, we will tell you and may refer you to a root canal specialist.

## Recovery and aftercare

The tooth and gum may be tender for a few days, especially when you bite. Over-the-counter pain relievers such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen, taken as the label directs, are enough for most people. Chew on the other side until the tooth has its final restoration.

Back teeth usually need a [crown](/services/crowns-and-bridges) after a root canal. Without the pulp, the tooth becomes more brittle, and a crown protects it from cracking. We aim to place it within a few weeks. Front teeth sometimes only need a filling.

Call us if swelling appears or grows, if pain gets worse after the first two or three days, or if your bite feels uneven.

## Cost and insurance

Costs vary with the tooth. A molar with several canals takes longer than a front tooth with one. Most PPO plans cover root canals as basic or major care for a share of the cost. We check your benefits and give you a written estimate before we begin, including the crown if you will need one. See [insurance and financing](/insurance-and-financing).

## Common questions

<details class="faq">
<summary>Does a root canal hurt?</summary>
<p>The treatment itself feels much like having a filling, because the tooth is numb. Most people say the toothache before the visit was far worse than the visit.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>Is it better to just pull the tooth?</summary>
<p>Keeping your own tooth is usually best for chewing and for the bone around it. A missing tooth also needs replacing, often with an implant or bridge, which costs more. We will still talk you through an <a href="/services/extractions">extraction</a> if you prefer it.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>How long does a tooth last after a root canal?</summary>
<p>With a well-fitted crown and good home care, a treated tooth can last many years, often for life. Delaying the crown is the most common reason they fail.</p>
</details>
