# How often should you get a dental cleaning?

<p class="byline">By <a href="/team/jenna-kowalski">Jenna Kowalski, RDH</a> · March 10, 2026</p>

<img src="/media/blog/how-often-should-you-get-a-dental-cleaning.jpg" alt="Hygiene tray with a mirror, a scaler and a small cup of polishing paste beside a reclined dental chair" width="800" height="500">

"Do I really need to come back in six months?" I hear that question almost every day. It is a fair one. The honest answer is that six months is a sensible default for most adults, but the right interval depends on what is happening in your mouth. Some people do best every three months. A few can safely come once a year.

## What a cleaning actually removes

Brushing and flossing remove soft plaque, the sticky film of bacteria that builds up every day. Plaque you miss starts to harden within a day or two. After that it becomes tartar, also called calculus, and no toothbrush can remove it. Tartar has a rough surface that collects even more plaque, and when it sits along or under the gumline it irritates the gums.

At a cleaning, your hygienist removes that tartar with an ultrasonic scaler and hand tools, then polishes and flosses. Just as important, we measure the small pocket around each tooth and look for changes since your last visit. A cleaning is part housekeeping and part early warning system.

## Why six months is the usual starting point

Gum problems tend to creep up slowly and without pain. Six months is often enough time for tartar to build up in the spots that are hard to reach, but usually not enough time for early gum inflammation to turn into lasting damage. Twice a year also means your dentist sees small cavities while they are still small, when a simple filling is enough.

For a healthy adult with good home care, that rhythm works well. But it is a starting point, not a rule.

## Who needs cleanings more often

We may suggest a visit every three or four months if any of these apply to you:

<ul class="check-list">
<li>You have been treated for gum disease. The bacteria that cause it can return to deep pockets in about three months, so shorter gaps help keep it in check.</li>
<li>You build tartar quickly. Some people simply do, often on the inside of the lower front teeth.</li>
<li>You smoke or vape. Both raise the risk of gum disease and can hide its warning signs, like bleeding.</li>
<li>You have diabetes. Gum disease and blood sugar affect each other, and keeping gums healthy is part of managing both.</li>
<li>You wear braces or have implants, bridges or crowns with edges that trap plaque.</li>
<li>You are pregnant. Hormone changes can make gums swollen and prone to bleeding.</li>
<li>You have a dry mouth from medication. Saliva protects teeth, and less of it means more plaque and more cavities.</li>
</ul>

More frequent visits are not a sign that you are doing something wrong. They are a way to give your gums a better chance while you work on the things you can control.

## Who can wait longer

If your gums have measured healthy for several years, you rarely build tartar and you have not had a new cavity in a long time, once a year can be enough. We only suggest this when your records support it, and we keep an eye on things in case anything changes.

## Signs you should not wait for your next visit

Some symptoms are worth a call before your next cleaning is due:

- Gums that bleed every time you brush or floss for more than two weeks
- Gums that look red, puffy or seem to be pulling away from your teeth
- Bad breath or a bad taste that does not go away
- A tooth that feels loose or a bite that suddenly feels different
- Sensitivity or pain that keeps coming back

## Making the time between visits count

The cleaning appointment matters, but what you do on the other 364 days matters more. Brush for two minutes twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste, and angle the bristles toward the gumline. Clean between your teeth once a day with floss, a floss pick or small interdental brushes, whichever you will actually use. Spit after brushing instead of rinsing, so the fluoride stays on your teeth a little longer. Keep sugary drinks to mealtimes rather than sipping all day.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>A quick self-check:</strong> if your gums bleed when you floss, keep flossing gently every day. Bleeding from plaque-related inflammation often settles within one to two weeks. If it does not, tell us.</p>
</div>

## Finding your interval

At your visit, your hygienist and dentist will look at your gum measurements, your history and how quickly you build tartar, then suggest an interval and explain why.
