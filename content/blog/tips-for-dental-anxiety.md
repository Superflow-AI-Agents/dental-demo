# Practical tips for dental anxiety

<p class="byline">By <a href="/team/dr-helen-whitfield">Dr. Helen Whitfield, DDS</a> · October 27, 2026</p>

<img src="/media/blog/tips-for-dental-anxiety.jpg" alt="A quiet treatment room with a dental chair facing a window, a soft blanket folded on the seat" width="800" height="500">

If the thought of a dental visit makes your heart race, you are in good company. Dental anxiety is very common, and it ranges from mild nerves the night before to a fear strong enough to keep people away for years. None of it is a character flaw. Often it traces back to a painful or embarrassing experience, a strong gag reflex or simply the feeling of lying back and not being in control.

The good news is that anxiety usually gets easier with the right approach. Here is what helps the patients I see.

## Before your visit

### Tell us when you book

This is the single most useful thing you can do. When we know you are nervous, we can set aside extra time, explain each step and plan the visit around you. You do not need to explain why. "I get really anxious at the dentist" is enough.

### Start small

If it has been a long time, your first appointment does not have to include any treatment. You can come in just to meet the dentist, talk through your worries and have a look around. Many people find the second visit much easier once the room and the faces are familiar.

### Choose your time wisely

Book a time when you are not rushed. For many people that is first thing in the morning, before there is a whole day to worry. Avoid lots of caffeine beforehand, since it can make a racing heart worse.

### Bring support

Bring someone you trust to sit in the waiting room or, if you like, in the room with you. Bring headphones and a playlist or a podcast you know well.

## During your visit

<ul class="check-list">
<li><strong>Agree on a stop signal.</strong> Raising your left hand is common. When you raise it, we stop. Knowing you can stop at any time gives back a sense of control.</li>
<li><strong>Ask us to explain as we go,</strong> or ask us not to, if details make it worse. Some people want a play-by-play, some prefer silence and music. Both are fine.</li>
<li><strong>Ask for breaks.</strong> A short pause to sit up, swallow or stretch can reset your nerves.</li>
<li><strong>Ask about numbing.</strong> We use numbing gel before injections. If you have felt pain at past visits, tell us, so we can wait longer and test before we begin.</li>
<li><strong>Mention a strong gag reflex.</strong> Small changes help, such as sitting more upright, smaller x-ray sensors or breathing through your nose.</li>
</ul>

## A breathing exercise that helps

Slow breathing is one of the most reliable ways to calm your body. Try this in the waiting room and in the chair:

1. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of four.
2. Hold for a count of two.
3. Breathe out slowly through your nose or mouth for a count of six.
4. Repeat five to ten times.

Making the out-breath longer than the in-breath signals your nervous system to settle. Practice it at home a few times before your visit, so it feels familiar when you need it.

You can also try tensing and relaxing muscle groups one at a time, starting with your feet and working up. Many people hold tension in their hands and shoulders without noticing.

## When nerves are stronger

For some people, practical tips are not enough. That is okay. Some patients benefit from medication to help them relax during treatment. Whether that is a good option depends on your health, your medications and the treatment planned, so talk it through with your dentist. If anxiety is severe or affects other parts of your life, a counselor who works with phobias can help too, often in just a few sessions.

## After your visit

Notice what went well, even if it was just walking through the door. Book your next visit before you leave, while the experience is fresh and you know what to expect. Regular, shorter visits are usually easier than rare, long ones, and catching problems early means simpler treatment.
