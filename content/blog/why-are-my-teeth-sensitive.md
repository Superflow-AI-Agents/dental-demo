# Why are my teeth sensitive?

<p class="byline">By <a href="/team/dr-marcus-oyelaran">Dr. Marcus Oyelaran, DMD</a> · July 7, 2026</p>

<img src="/media/blog/why-are-my-teeth-sensitive.jpg" alt="A glass of iced water with a soft-bristled toothbrush and a tube of toothpaste on a white counter" width="800" height="500">

You take a sip of ice water or bite into ice cream and feel a quick, sharp zing. Tooth sensitivity is one of the most common things patients mention at checkups. The good news is that it is usually manageable. The key is figuring out what is causing it.

## What is happening inside the tooth

The outer layer of a tooth crown is enamel, the hardest material in your body. Below the gumline, the root is covered by a thinner layer called cementum. Under both is dentin, which is full of tiny tubes that lead toward the nerve in the center of the tooth.

When enamel wears thin or gums pull back and expose the root, those tubes are left open. Cold, heat, sweet or sour foods and even a breath of cold air can then move the fluid inside the tubes and trigger the nerve. The result is a short, sharp pain that fades once the trigger is gone.

## Common causes

- **Gum recession.** As gums pull back, the root surface is exposed. Root surfaces are much more sensitive than enamel. Recession can come from gum disease, brushing too hard or simply age.
- **Brushing too hard.** A hard-bristled brush or heavy scrubbing can wear away gums and the enamel near the gumline over the years.
- **Acid wear.** Frequent soda, sports drinks, citrus, wine or vinegar can soften and dissolve enamel. So can acid reflux or frequent vomiting.
- **Grinding or clenching.** Many people grind at night without knowing it. Over time this can wear enamel and create small cracks.
- **Cavities or a broken filling.** Decay opens a path into the dentin.
- **A cracked tooth.** This often causes sharp pain when you bite down or release your bite.
- **After dental work.** A new filling, a crown or a whitening treatment can make a tooth sensitive for a few days to a few weeks. This usually settles on its own.

## What you can do at home

For mild sensitivity, these steps often help within a few weeks:

<ul class="check-list">
<li>Switch to a soft-bristled toothbrush and use gentle strokes. If you use an electric brush, one with a pressure sensor helps.</li>
<li>Use a toothpaste made for sensitive teeth, with ingredients such as potassium nitrate or stannous fluoride. Use it twice a day, every day. It can take two to four weeks to feel the full effect.</li>
<li>At bedtime, rub a little of that toothpaste onto the sensitive spot and do not rinse it off.</li>
<li>Spit after brushing instead of rinsing with water.</li>
<li>Keep acidic foods and drinks to mealtimes, and rinse with water afterward.</li>
<li>Wait about 30 minutes after something acidic before you brush, so you are not scrubbing softened enamel.</li>
<li>Drink cold or acidic drinks through a straw.</li>
</ul>

## What we can do in the office

If home care is not enough, there are several options, depending on the cause:

- **Fluoride varnish or a desensitizing coating** painted onto exposed roots to seal the tiny tubes.
- **Bonding,** a thin layer of tooth-colored resin over an exposed root or worn area.
- **A night guard** if grinding is the cause.
- **A filling or crown** to repair decay, a cracked tooth or a leaking old filling.
- **A gum graft** for significant recession, to cover and protect the root.
- **Root canal treatment** in the less common case where the nerve itself is inflamed and will not recover.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Sensitivity versus a problem tooth:</strong> normal sensitivity is a quick twinge that fades within a few seconds. Pain that lingers for 30 seconds or more after something hot or cold, throbs on its own, wakes you at night or comes with swelling is different. Please call us soon.</p>
</div>

## When to see your dentist

Make an appointment if:

- Sensitivity has not improved after a few weeks of using a sensitivity toothpaste
- It is limited to one tooth
- It hurts to bite down
- You see a dark spot, hole or chip in a tooth
- Your gums look like they are receding
