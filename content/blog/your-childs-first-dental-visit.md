# Your child's first dental visit: what to expect

<p class="byline">By <a href="/team/dr-priya-natarajan">Dr. Priya Natarajan, DDS</a> · May 19, 2026</p>

<img src="/media/blog/your-childs-first-dental-visit.jpg" alt="Small toothbrushes in bright colors and a stuffed animal on a counter in a kids treatment room" width="800" height="500">

Many parents are surprised when I tell them their baby should see a dentist by their first birthday. One year old can feel very early for a mouth with only a few teeth. But that first visit is less about fixing problems and more about preventing them, and about helping your child learn that the dental office is a friendly place.

## When to book the first visit

The usual guidance is to book a first visit within six months of the first tooth appearing, and no later than the first birthday. If your child is older than that and has not been yet, that is fine. Book now. It is never too late to start.

You should call sooner if you notice white or brown spots on the teeth, chipped or discolored teeth after a fall, or if your child seems to have pain when eating.

## What happens at the visit

A first visit is short, usually 20 to 30 minutes, and most of it is talking with you.

1. **Getting to know each other.** We ask about your child's health, feeding, bottle or sippy cup habits, and any family history of cavities.
2. **A knee-to-knee exam.** For babies and toddlers, you sit facing the dentist with your child on your lap, then gently lay them back so their head rests on our knees. Your child can see you the whole time. We count teeth, check the gums, tongue and bite, and look for early signs of decay.
3. **A gentle cleaning, if needed.** For many young children this is just a quick brush with a soft toothbrush.
4. **Fluoride varnish.** We may paint a thin coat of fluoride on the teeth. It takes seconds and helps harden enamel.
5. **Your questions.** We talk about teething, brushing, thumb sucking, pacifiers and anything else on your mind.

X-rays are rarely needed at a first visit. We only take them when there is a specific reason.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Crying is normal.</strong> Many toddlers cry during an exam, simply because a stranger is looking in their mouth. It does not mean anything hurts, and it does not mean the visit went badly. We have seen it all and we will not rush.</p>
</div>

## How to prepare your child

A little preparation goes a long way.

- **Pick a good time of day.** Morning appointments, or any time after a nap, usually go better than late afternoon.
- **Keep your words simple and positive.** Say "the dentist will count your teeth" rather than "it will not hurt." Even well-meant words like "shot" or "pain" can plant worry.
- **Play dentist at home.** Take turns counting each other's teeth, or check a favorite stuffed animal's teeth with a toothbrush.
- **Read a picture book** about going to the dentist in the week before.
- **Stay calm yourself.** Children read our faces. If you have your own dental worries, try not to share them on the way in.
- **Bring a comfort item** like a small toy or blanket.

## Caring for baby teeth at home

Baby teeth matter. They help children chew, speak clearly and hold space for adult teeth. A few habits protect them:

<ul class="check-list">
<li>Wipe your baby's gums with a soft, damp cloth after feeds, even before teeth appear.</li>
<li>Once teeth come in, brush twice a day with a soft, small toothbrush.</li>
<li>Use a smear of fluoride toothpaste the size of a grain of rice until age 3, then a pea-sized amount from age 3 to 6.</li>
<li>Do not put your child to bed with a bottle of milk, formula or juice. Water is fine.</li>
<li>Keep juice and sweet snacks to mealtimes instead of sipping and snacking all day.</li>
<li>Brush for your child until they can tie their own shoes, usually around age 6 to 8, and keep checking their work after that.</li>
</ul>

## After the first visit

Most children then come back every six months. Regular visits mean we can spot small cavities early, apply fluoride and sealants when they make sense, and keep an eye on how teeth and jaws are growing. Just as importantly, each visit builds familiarity, so the dental chair becomes a normal part of life.
