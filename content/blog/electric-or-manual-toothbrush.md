# Electric or manual toothbrush: which is better?

<p class="byline">By <a href="/team/luis-ortega">Luis Ortega, RDH</a> · April 21, 2026</p>

<img src="/media/blog/electric-or-manual-toothbrush.jpg" alt="An electric toothbrush on its charging base next to a manual toothbrush in a cup on a bathroom counter" width="800" height="500">

Patients ask me this at nearly every cleaning, usually while I am looking at the spots their brush has been missing. Here is the short answer: a manual toothbrush used well does a good job, and an electric toothbrush makes doing a good job easier for most people. The best brush is the one you use for two full minutes, twice a day, with the right technique.

## What the research generally shows

Comparisons over the years tend to find that powered brushes, especially ones with a small round head that rotates and oscillates, remove a bit more plaque and lead to a bit less gum inflammation than manual brushes. The difference is real but modest. It is not a reason to feel guilty about your manual brush if your gums are healthy and your checkups go well.

Where electric brushes really help is in making good habits automatic. They time your brushing, they do the scrubbing motion for you, and many warn you when you press too hard.

## When an electric toothbrush is worth it

I tend to recommend an electric brush for people who:

<ul class="check-list">
<li>Have arthritis, a hand injury or anything else that makes small, precise movements hard</li>
<li>Wear braces, where brackets and wires create many hiding places for plaque</li>
<li>Have early gum inflammation and want a little extra help along the gumline</li>
<li>Brush too hard and are starting to see gum recession or worn enamel near the gums</li>
<li>Tend to rush and rarely brush for the full two minutes</li>
<li>Are kids who need a bit of fun to get them to brush</li>
</ul>

## When a manual brush is perfectly fine

If your gums do not bleed, your hygienist is not finding much tartar and you have no trouble reaching every surface, a manual brush is a perfectly good choice. Manual brushes are cheap, need no charging, travel well and are easy to replace. Many people with healthy mouths have used one their whole lives.

## Features that matter (and ones that do not)

You do not need the most expensive model. Look for:

- **Soft bristles.** This applies to manual and electric brushes alike. Medium and hard bristles do not clean better, and they can wear down gums and enamel over time.
- **A built-in two-minute timer,** ideally one that pauses every 30 seconds so you move to the next quarter of your mouth.
- **A pressure sensor** that lights up or slows down when you push too hard.
- **Replacement heads that are easy to find** at a price you will actually pay every three months.

App connections, many cleaning modes and fancy cases are nice extras, but they do not clean your teeth.

## Technique matters more than the brush

Whichever brush you choose, these habits make the biggest difference:

1. **Aim for the gumline.** Tilt the bristles at about 45 degrees toward where your teeth meet your gums. That is where plaque does the most harm.
2. **Go gently.** With a manual brush, use small circles or short back-and-forth strokes. With an electric brush, simply guide it slowly from tooth to tooth and let it do the work. No scrubbing.
3. **Take the full two minutes.** Most people brush for well under a minute without realizing it.
4. **Do not forget the inside surfaces.** The tongue side of the lower front teeth and the cheek side of the upper back teeth are the spots I see missed most.
5. **Spit, do not rinse.** Leaving a thin layer of fluoride toothpaste on your teeth helps protect them.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Replace your brush or brush head every three months,</strong> or sooner if the bristles splay out. Splayed bristles clean poorly. If yours look worn after a few weeks, you are probably pressing too hard.</p>
</div>

## Do not skip between your teeth

No toothbrush, electric or manual, cleans well between teeth. About a third of each tooth's surface is in those tight spaces. Floss, floss picks, small interdental brushes or a water flosser can all work. Pick the one you will use every day.
